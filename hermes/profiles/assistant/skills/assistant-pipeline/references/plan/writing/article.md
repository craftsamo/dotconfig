# Article / long-form prose — decision surface

Text read start-to-finish: technical articles, blog posts,
tutorials, book chapters, narrative explainers. The writer owns
argumentation and pacing craft (its Japanese norms stack); you own
what the piece claims and for whom.

Writer type `technical-prose` · QA `prose` · units: outline unit
first, then piece units per section/chapter for long work; whole
small job for a short post.

## Fix before release

- **The claim** — the one-line payoff the title will promise; an
  article that cannot state its claim in one line is not ready to
  brief.
- **Audience knowledge level** — what the reader already knows;
  decides depth, terminology, and how much ground the opening must
  cover.
- **Shape & size** — single article vs series vs chapter of a
  larger work (a series briefed as "one article" is a granularity
  finding); target length range.
- **Source pack** — pasted research conclusions and product facts
  for every factual claim the piece will make; tutorials
  additionally need the verified steps (commands actually run,
  outputs observed) — the writer formats them, it does not verify
  them.
- **Tone decision path** — settled register + samples, or
  explicitly deferred to the outline unit's 2-3 opening samples.
- **Done criteria** — e.g. "a reader at level X can do Y after
  reading".

## Defaults

- Anything beyond a short post gets the outline unit first —
  structure approval before prose is the cheap gate.
- Tutorials: steps in execution order, each with its observable
  result (the brief supplies both).
- Book/series work: one piece unit per chapter, outline approved at
  the work level first; chapter order respects dependency, not
  writing convenience.

## Red flags

- Factual claims expected with no source pack — the integrity floor
  will strike them; the gap is in the brief, not the draft.
- "Make it engaging" as the only direction — the claim and audience
  are undecided.
- A tutorial briefed from imagined steps nobody ran.
- Restructuring feedback given on a full draft that never had an
  outline unit — the process failure is yours, not the writer's.
