# Analyzing a report

Examine how the report connects its question, period/population, method,
observations, interpretation and limitations. Check these only to the depth
the actual question requires; not every status note is a research study.

Anchor a claimed contradiction or unsupported inference to the relevant
numbers and wording. A difference between periods may reflect definition
changes; do not assume comparability. Source availability is distinct from
the truth of the underlying result or a reproduced calculation.

## Establish a finding

For each material finding, quote the claim, identify the relevant source or
other passage, explain the mismatch and its consequence for the report's
question, and state what needs checking or correcting. Classify a supplied
source mismatch separately from an unavailable source and from optional style.
Do not turn the list of checks into a compulsory report template.

## Worked example and counterexample

Fictional teaching material: "60% of customers are satisfied" appears in the
summary, while the supplied table records 30 satisfied respondents out of 50
and gives no total customer population. The finding is an unsupported expansion
from respondents to customers; cite both passages. The reader could mistake
the sample result for a population estimate. Recommend aligning the scope or
obtaining the missing population evidence, not a complete replacement report.

If the table is unavailable, report that the population claim cannot be checked;
do not declare the 60% false. "The change may explain the decline" is not a
causation error merely because it considers a possible explanation. A report
that accurately describes status needs no invented defect about a missing CTA.

QA: distinguish source facts, your interpretation and unknowns. Do not infer
causation, demand a recommendation for a descriptive report, or present the
analysis report as a newly performed investigation.
