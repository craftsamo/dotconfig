# Proposal and decision memo

Identify the decision or discussion the requester wants to enable, its scope,
known alternatives and constraints. A requested recommendation needs the
requester's position or enough authorized evidence to explain a proposal;
an open comparison should not be converted into a settled recommendation.

Explain the problem, the relevant options and their trade-offs using common
criteria. Separate known costs and effects from estimates or assumptions.
State unresolved questions alongside what can already be decided. Match the
amount of background to what is needed for this decision, not a fixed outline.

Do not invent personal experience, a preferred option, priorities or a phased
rollout. Proposed action is not approval, and an illustrative schedule is not
a commitment. If the intended decision is unclear, ask instead of filling
the memo with generic arguments.

## Make the decision comparable

Open with the decision sought and its boundary, then the supplied provisional
stance if one exists. Give reasons and trade-offs before implementation detail
that the decision does not yet authorize. A discussion paper may end with a
well-defined open choice; these are guides, not a compulsory recommendation
template or permission to invent the author's preference.

Compare alternatives on the same axes, with the same units and assumptions.
Do not contrast one option's cost with another's convenience as if that ranks
them. Explain which supplied condition makes a trade-off matter. Unknown
costs stay unknown; estimates are not budgets, and a tentative preference is
not a winner. Do not introduce an implementation phase to manufacture closure.

## Worked example and retain condition

Fictional teaching material: a team asks whether to pilot live workshops or
recorded lessons. Live sessions allow immediate questions but need a shared
time; recordings allow asynchronous access but questions wait for a reply.
Costs are not supplied. The requester leans toward recordings if delayed
answers are acceptable; this is not an approval.

A decision passage can read: "Decide which training format to pilot.
On access, live workshops need a shared time; recordings are asynchronous.
On feedback, workshops allow immediate questions; recordings delay answers.
The requester's provisional preference is recordings if delayed answers are
acceptable. Costs remain unknown, so this memo cannot support a cost ranking."
Common axes expose the real trade-off and keep the condition on the stance.

Retain an open comparison when no preference was supplied. "Recordings win
because they are cheaper" would invent both cost evidence and a decision.
A missing budget need not prevent a discussion limited to access and feedback.

QA: judge the released decision or comparison, including conditions on any
stance; do not demand a rollout plan for an exploratory choice.

QA: readers can identify what is being proposed or compared, on what evidence,
and what remains open. The conclusion does not silently outrun the material
or turn an exploratory memo into an authorization.
