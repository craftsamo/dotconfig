# Editing a report

Preserve numbers with their units, observation periods, populations, methods
and limitations. Moving a summary must not leave its essential qualification
behind. Correcting a number or comparison needs the corresponding source.

Keep observation, inference and recommendation distinct. Do not strengthen
"may explain" to "caused", weaken a verified finding without reason, or infer
a missing population definition to make two periods comparable.

## Decide what to change

Read the requested change first. For wording edits, repair the specific
dependency, repetition or misleading scope without redesigning the report.
For authorized structure edits, move the answer and the qualification together;
keep the definitions needed to understand a table beside that table. Compare
the headline, summary and body after any move so none becomes more certain.
If the sentence is already accurate and usable, leave it unchanged.

## Worked example and counterexample

Fictional teaching material: the source says 30 of 50 respondents were satisfied
and gives no customer population. A draft says "60% of customers are satisfied."
Within an authorized accuracy/wording correction, change the population to
"30 of the 50 respondents (60%) reported satisfaction." If changing the
claim is protected or outside scope, flag the conflict instead of silently
changing it. Never guess the response rate to make the summary look complete.

Retain "The change may explain the decline" when the material supplies only
a possible explanation; do not polish it into "The change caused the decline."
A descriptive report does not become defective because it has no recommendation.
Missing source data warrants a named uncertainty, not a guessed correction.

QA: summary and body agree after the edit, evidence still supports the stated
scope, and negation/uncertainty survive. Do not claim to have reproduced data
collection, queries or calculations that were only supplied as text.
