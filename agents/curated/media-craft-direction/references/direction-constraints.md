# Direction and constraints

Read when selecting an expression, choosing among concepts, or deciding what must
survive a revision. A style label is not an explanation of why a concept works.

## Choose an organizing idea

Connect the audience's starting point to the intended change. A novice learning a
process needs orientation and visible consequences; an audience recognizing a
brand may need a distinctive silhouette; a short notification needs a clear event
without a long emotional arc. This is not a universal preference for simplicity:
a richly layered poster can deliberately reward sustained attention.

Describe a concept through a relationship: a small event grows into an overview,
one repeated mark changes meaning, a spoken correction leaves room for relief, or
a recurring musical cell returns in a new register. Describe decoration after
that relationship, not instead of it.

Compare plausible alternatives on the same purpose, constraints and risk. Do not
present a deliberately weak straw option beside a favored one. If the direction
is settled and feasible, proceed within the host's authorization rather than
inventing another approval round.

## Protect the right things

Distinguish exact words, meaning, identity, timing, geometry, original files and
output pixels. A source file may need to stay unchanged while an authorized new
composition uses a copy. A logo may require geometric fidelity. A character may
allow new poses but not changes to identifying features. A named voice may bind
both performer identity and engine. Never infer which of these can change from
the fact that a new look was requested.

If previously approved pixels conflict with a new visual direction, name the
tradeoff and request the necessary scope. Do not solve it by quietly reskinning
an immutable product, nor by insisting that every earlier aesthetic decision is
permanent when the client only protected the content.

## Worked case: a catalog becomes a launch card

**Request.** Keep approved product names and prices, but give a product card a
more confident editorial treatment. No requirement fixes the prior layout.

**Weak choice.** Preserve the old table as a bitmap and add a shadow, tilt and
glow. Its cramped typography and uniform emphasis remain unchanged.

**Diagnosis.** Content fidelity was confused with pixel fidelity. The new shell
cannot establish hierarchy inside the old image.

**Revision.** Propose a reauthored hierarchy using the same exact names and prices:
one primary product image, a distinct price role and quiet supporting conditions.
Keep the original asset unchanged. If the bitmap itself is explicitly immutable,
show the limitation and offer an authorized framing-only option instead.

**Evidence.** Compare the first reading and product/price association at use size.
Check exact copy separately; neither a hash nor an attractive shell settles both.

## Worked case: "maybe more cinematic"

**Request.** A client suggests that an explanatory UI sequence could be more
cinematic, while retaining accurate controls and clear task results.

**Weak choice.** Treat the suggestion as an instruction for constant camera
movement, dramatic lighting and a new transition at every cut.

**Diagnosis.** A tentative adjective became multiple mandatory techniques. The
effects compete with the control the viewer is meant to notice.

**Revision.** Propose selective framing and a contrast between approach, action
and result. Keep the UI stable while the meaningful state changes. Treat camera
movement as one candidate, not as the definition of cinematic quality.

**Evidence.** Review the action at normal speed and inspect its contact/result
frames. The viewer should recognize what changed without chasing the camera.

## Retain cases

A symmetrical, uniformly weighted mark can be exactly right for a quiet identity.
A mechanical voice can be intentional characterization. A static held frame can
be the strongest result after a busy sequence. Do not "improve" these into a
default expressive style when they already satisfy the decided purpose.
