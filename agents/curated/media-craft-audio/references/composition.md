# Composition

Craft feedback on melody, harmony, and phrase structure for an original score
or cue — regardless of genre. These are structural techniques, not a style
preference, and apply the same whether the requested result is orchestral,
electronic, folk, or anything else.

## Motif and register

A **motif** is a short, memorable pitch/rhythm cell (often 2-4 notes) that
gives a listener something to hold onto and recognize when it returns. A
melody with no repeated or developed motif tends to feel aimless even if
every individual note is "correct," because nothing in it is memorable.

**Register** is where a line sits in pitch range, and it carries connotation:
a low register reads as weighty, ominous, or grounded; a high register reads
as light, tense, or exposed. Moving a motif to a different register when it
returns (transposed up or down) is a common way to develop it without simply
repeating it verbatim.

## Rhythm and phrasing

Group notes into **phrases** with a clear beginning and end — usually a
consistent or intentionally-varied length (e.g., regular 4-bar phrases, or a
deliberate 3+5 asymmetry for a specific effect) rather than random,
unpredictable lengths with no discernible grouping. Random phrase lengths
with no cadence at the end of each one are a common cause of a melody
"wandering" even when the pitches themselves are fine.

## Harmonic movement: common-tone, stepwise, contrast, endings

Three ways adjacent chords can relate, useful for diagnosing why a
progression feels smooth, tense, or sloppy:

- **Common-tone movement**: the new chord shares at least one note with the
  previous chord, giving a smooth bridge (e.g., C major [C-E-G] to A minor
  [A-C-E] shares C and E).
- **Stepwise movement**: moving voices use adjacent scale steps rather than
  leaps. This can coexist with common tones held in other voices.
- **Contrast**: a deliberate leap or unrelated harmony used for tension or a
  narrative turn. This reads as intentional when it's the only place such a
  leap occurs; it reads as sloppy when leaps like it happen throughout with
  no other smooth movement to contrast against.

**Endings/cadences**: in a tonal brief, how a phrase resolves can signal whether it's "done."
A stepwise descent back to the tonic reads as a clear resolution; ending a
phrase on an unresolved or unexpected note reads as intentionally open only
if the surrounding material makes clear that openness was the point. Ambient,
nonfunctional and through-composed music need not follow this tonal pattern.

## Worked case: aimless melody

**Request**: "This melody for a synth lead doesn't stick with me."

**Observed** (key of C major, basic synth patch, quarter note = 1 beat): a
short motif appears once at the very start (C4-E4-D4, up a third then down a
step, over 2 beats) and never returns; subsequent phrases are 3, 7, and 2
beats long with no repeated shape and no clear point of resolution.

**Cause**: the motif is introduced but never repeated or developed, so the
ear has nothing recurring to latch onto; irregular, unresolved phrase lengths
never let the listener sense where one musical thought ends and the next
begins.

**Revision**: restate the opening motif (transposed) at the start of each
subsequent 4-beat phrase — e.g., the second phrase begins F4-A4-G4 (the same
shape, a fourth higher). For a closing phrase that needs tonal resolution,
try E4-D4-C4; do not force every phrase to close on the tonic.

## Worked case: wandering chord pad

**Request**: "This chord pad for an ambient bed needs smoother transitions
between chords."

**Observed**: chords are chosen with no relationship to their neighbors —
C major, then F# major, then Ab major — each jumping to a distant register with no
shared tones and no stepwise voice movement between them.

**Cause**: no common-tone or stepwise voice leading connects the chords; each
chord's voicing is recalculated from scratch rather than moved from the
previous one, producing large, unmotivated leaps throughout.

**Revision**: where possible, keep at least one note in common between
neighboring chords (C major to A minor keeps C and E); where a genuine
harmonic contrast is wanted for a specific narrative turn, use a
chromatic semitone bridge instead of an unrelated leap (e.g., G to G# to A) so the
contrast reads as a deliberate choice rather than a mistake.

## When no revision applies

**Request**: general note that "the melody repeats a lot" on a cue explicitly
built as a short, seamlessly loopable ambient bed for a game menu.

**Observation**: the motif's repetition is the intended function here — a
loopable bed needs to avoid a strong forward-moving structure that would make
the loop point audible — and the cadence at the loop point is deliberately
soft rather than a strong resolution, by design.

**Response**: state that the repetition serves the stated loop requirement
and isn't a defect in this context; no revision applies unless the loop
length or use case changes.
