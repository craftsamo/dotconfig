# Continuity Across Cuts

Continuity is the discipline of keeping a subject's position, direction, and
motion legible when the shot changes — not a rule that every cut must be
smoothed. Two related but distinct kinds of continuity come up in produced
motion: position continuity (where the subject sits in frame from one shot
to the next) and motion-vector continuity (whether an object already moving
in one direction keeps that same apparent direction into the next shot, as
in a true match cut). Decide, for each cut, whether continuity is the intent
of that transition at all before applying either kind.

## Deliberate cuts are a legitimate tool, not a defect

A hard cut with no positional or motion match is often the correct choice:
it signals a scene change, a tonal break, an elapsed interval, or a
deliberate juxtaposition. Montage sequences, music videos cut to a beat, and
ads that intentionally jolt between disconnected images all rely on cuts
that do not match position or motion — forcing a cross-dissolve or a
motion-matched transition onto footage designed to jolt would undo the
intended effect. The judgment call is not "should this cut be smoothed" but
"is this cut trying to preserve the viewer's spatial sense of one continuous
world, or trying to juxtapose two different ones" — only the former needs
matching.

## When continuity is the intent, match the anchor

When a cut is meant to feel continuous (the viewer should track a single
subject or idea across the edit), match the anchor the eye is tracking:
keep the subject's screen position close between the outgoing and incoming
frame, keep established screen direction (a subject moving right should not
reappear moving left with no cause), or carry a motion vector through the
cut so the movement itself bridges the two shots. A cut that was clearly
meant to feel continuous but drops the anchor — the subject jumps to an
unrelated part of the frame with no bridging move — reads as a mistake
rather than a choice, because the viewer has no way to tell it was
intentional.

## Avoid idle drift

Idle drift is motion left running for no narrative reason — a looping
ambient animation, a subtle background bob, or a particle field that
continues at constant amplitude regardless of what is happening on screen.
It is not automatically wrong for something to idle (a loading spinner or a
subtly breathing background can correctly communicate "still active" or add
texture) — the distinction is whether the idle motion is doing a
communicative job or is simply left on because nothing told it to stop.
Idle motion that competes with a foreground message (moving background
behind text the viewer needs to read, animation that continues at full
amplitude under a static diagram) is drift regardless of how subtle it is,
because it costs attention without earning it.

## Cases

**Revision — lost anchor on a product tour.** Request: a product tour cuts
from a wide shot of a device to a close-up of the same button, but the
button sits in a completely different part of the frame in the close-up with
no bridging camera move. Weak observation: viewers lose track of what they
are looking at for a beat after the cut. Cause: the cut was clearly intended
to feel like "zooming into" the same object, but nothing anchors the two
frames together — no positional match, no camera move bridging the two
scales. Revision: either reposition the close-up so the button occupies a
similar screen region as in the wide shot, or add a brief camera push that
carries the eye from the wide framing into the close-up.

**Revision — idle background under an explainer diagram.** Request: an
explainer video keeps a soft particle-drift background animating at full,
constant amplitude behind a static diagram the narrator is walking through.
Weak observation: viewers report the diagram feels "busy" and harder to
read than the content warrants. Cause: the background loop is a literal,
unmodulated ambient template with no relationship to the foreground content
— it never yields attention while the diagram needs to be read. Revision:
reduce the background's amplitude and/or pause it while the diagram is the
active focus, resuming it only during transitions between diagram states.

**Retain — beat-cut juxtaposition in a music video.** Request/observation: a
music video cuts hard, on the beat, between two entirely different locations
with no motion match or positional bridge; a reviewer initially flags this
as a continuity break. Evidence for retaining it: the cut lands exactly on
an identified beat marker in the track, and juxtaposing unrelated locations
on the beat is a standard, intentional convention of the genre — the video
is not trying to preserve one continuous space. No positional or motion
match is added; the deliberate disjunction is the point of the edit.
